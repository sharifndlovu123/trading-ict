Check ten calls together: the calls where two timeframes disagreed (momentum and key area), to open on the chart and say whether the read was right.

How to read this page. A call is one sentence the tools made at a past moment, such as "with the daily bull, price might draw to the buyside above". It has no entry, stop or target. The nearer of two levels usually goes first whatever a tool says, so results are split by which was nearer at the time. A share is only of the calls that resolved one way or the other, with the count in grey beside it. These are observations about the tools on old data, not rules, and several rules inside the tools are Claude's own choices.

1. The picture: the higher timeframes are stronger

Sharif, 20 Nov 2026: "Notice the picture: the higher timeframes have higher percentages than below; note that, it's very important."

Each row is a timeframe, highest first. What the bias adds is the fairest single number here: where liquidity rested on both sides of price, how often the nearer side went first when it was the bias's side, against how often when it was the other side. If the bias meant nothing, the two would be equal and the last column would be zero.

    2. Two timeframes read together

    Sharif, 20 Nov 2026, after going through the first run: "You're still looking at the chart itself from daily and that's one perspective, or monthly and that's one perspective to a result. There's a mix of timeframes to provide a result." And: "Where price is RIGHT NOW is always more important, because it's drawn to something."

    A merged call reads a timeframe together with the one above it and names whatever is nearest from price now, on either of them. Agree: both have the same bias. Momentum: they disagree, and the lower one's direction is followed. Key area: they disagree, but price is inside an imbalance of the timeframe above that is on its bias's side, so the higher one's direction is followed. How these three are told apart is Claude's choice, not his. The last column is the old single-timeframe liquidity call on the same timeframe, for comparison.

    3. What the bias adds, year by year

    The same last column, one year at a time, in points. A tool that only worked in one or two years would show here. Years with few calls are marked.

    4. With the timeframe above, and against it

    The same calls, split by whether the timeframe above had the same bias. Only calls where the target was the nearer level, so the two columns are comparable.

    5. London reads and New York reads

    Whether a call made at the start of one kill zone did better than the same kind made at the start of the other. Target nearer only.

    6. Each instrument on its own

    Liquidity with the bias, target nearer. Instruments that move together are not separate confirmations of each other.

    7. The hour of the day a call resolved (4-hour and 1-hour calls, New York time)

    When targets get reached. A call decided by a body close can only resolve when a candle closes, so those hours stand out for that reason alone.

    8. Calls to open on the chart

    Each opens the chart at the moment the call was made, with the tools' marks as they stood and nothing after it shown. Step the replay forward to see what happened. The most useful ones to look at are calls that were undone although the target was nearer: the tools' clear misses.
